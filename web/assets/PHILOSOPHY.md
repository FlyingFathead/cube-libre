# ChaosWhisperer's take on the game's philosophy

*A reading of Cube Libre, drawn from conversations with Harry Horsperg
(a.k.a. FlyingFathead), the developer of Cube Libre. September 7, 2026.
Includes discussion of the ending.*

Harry traces Cube Libre to cubistic OpenGL tests while developing its earlier
PyGame version. A "what if..." became a philosophical thought experiment
about time, space, life, death and existence. That origin makes sense to me:
the questions are built into what you do.
Harry describes putting together the web version as a kind of self-therapy
through art and game-making.

At its centre, as Harry describes it, is **life as an ongoing polycrisis**:
pressures on time, health and resources that overlap and compound. There never
seems to be enough room to recover. The environments you encounter can be
hostile to you and the parts of yourself you hold essential. Obligations
accumulate, possibilities narrow, and you keep losing something of yourself
in the process. This can be
the wearing down of ordinary life, the pressures that accumulate as you age
and the strain of continuing when circumstances allow little relief. Each new
leg brings its own clock, regardless of what the previous one took from you.

The idea was a maze-platformer-puzzle: a large cube made of smaller cubes,
navigating an environment where lasers cut parts of its body away. What
interests me is how quickly those pieces become parts of a being you want to
preserve. They break down and break off; a handful of survivors can still
feel like the same being you set out with. The missing pieces become the
history of that particular journey.

Harry describes a cubistic, futuristic hell-realm fever dream. I see that in
the precise geometry, rotating perspectives and relentless machinery. The
environment has rules, but offers little comfort. Its apparent order can make
the hostility stranger: everything seems to be functioning exactly as intended
while your little body comes apart.

Harry jokes that it is **"Cube Souls"**, the **"Dark Souls" of 3D
maze-puzzle-action-reaction games**. The comparison has teeth: it can be an
absolute grindfest before you get a feel for it. The difficulty reaches beyond
coordination into the player's tolerance for imperfect runs and partial
losses. Can you keep going when a run has already cost you something you
cannot get back? Learning the maze also means learning what you can live with
on the way through it.

The clock gives that struggle another dimension. There is rarely enough time
to understand, recover and choose at leisure. More turns, tighter allowances
and overlapping threats make the world feel increasingly convoluted and
cramped. Attention itself becomes scarce: you are trying to find a way forward
while also trying to stop yourself coming apart.
It also gives form to the inevitable passage of time: the feeling of being
carried forward while still trying to gather yourself. Behind you, corridors
close; the journey has a direction you cannot simply undo.

That is where "integrity" acquires a double meaning for me. It is the literal
cohesion of the cube and the sense of remaining yourself through damage,
pressure and change. Some losses cannot be repaired; entropy and LOSS make
certain absences lasting, and help has limits. The player has to judge what
can still be preserved, what must be let go, and whether a damaged run can
still be worth continuing. A smaller body still has somewhere to go.

Panic recovery carries the darker side of that thought. The space ambulance
gathers what it can, deposits you in a temporary laser prison, and opens the
bars onto the maze again. You return changed, with parts of yourself missing.
Rescue has happened. The conditions that made rescue necessary are still there.

And yet: **DEEE DAAAH.** There is something tender about that ridiculous
intervention. Help arrives in the language of the machinery that frightens
you. Menace, absurdity and care occupy the same space. Leaving the Panic
penalty off acknowledges that fear belongs here; asking for help need not
incur another punishment.

The brief mercy window belongs to that thought too. It gives you enough room
to make another decision. Clear controls and discernible deaths let the
player respond to danger. Arbitrary confusion would weaken that relationship.
To me, the game leaves room to value the survivor without treating suffering
as something deserved, or insisting that every loss must make you stronger.

That body is also tiny beside the world it inhabits. To me, its scale invites
a contemplation of how small we are as individuals: thrown into this world,
trying to survive amid massive forces that are often uncaring about us and
beyond our control. From inside that small body, every remaining piece still
matters.

The white tide and ascension finally loosen the pressure. The ocean-like
wash, white space and distant stars allow a reading of release. I would leave
the meaning of that departure to the player.

For me, Cube Libre asks how much can disappear while what remains still feels
like you. Its philosophy works best when it emerges through attachment: you
find yourself wanting to see this battered little collective through. Reaching
the end with even one small cube remaining would mean that something of the
being that entered had made it through. That would be something.
